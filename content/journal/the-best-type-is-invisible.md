### Two faces, no more

Every Mira project starts with the same type decision: one workhorse for body copy that reads for hours without effort, and one display face with enough personality to hold a headline. That is the whole system. No third face for accents. No decorative flourishes borrowed from moodboards. No condensed variant just because it looked good on Pinterest.

Two faces is not a rule of taste. It is a rule of maintenance. Buyers of design templates and clients handing off brand systems always drift toward complexity. Every extra face is one more thing to police, one more chance for the system to break the moment a junior designer picks a heading style.

### Restraint at the type layer earns loudness everywhere else

When type stays quiet, everything around it gets to be loud. Bigger images. Bolder statements. More confident whitespace. This is the trade we make on every project. The typography is doing the invisible work of holding voice, so the rest of the design can push harder in the visible directions.

The best sites we have shipped are the ones where the reader could not name the typeface if you asked them. They only remember how the site felt. That is type doing its job.

### Where most brands go wrong

Most brands over-select type. They pick a wordmark font, a headline font, a subheading font, a body font, and a monospace accent, and they think the layered specificity is a strength. It is not. It is a system with five points of failure and no single voice.

Restraint is the harder move. Pick two. Use them everywhere. Let the tone come from what you write and where you place it, not from how many typefaces you rotated through.

### What invisible looks like

Invisible does not mean bland. It means legible, distinctive, and unremarkable in the right places. Good typography feels natural. You feel it before you see it. If a client asks us why we chose a particular font, we usually answer by asking them how the site felt to read. That is the whole test.
